## Chapter 9: Pharmacologic Approaches to Glycemic Treatment

## Pharmacologic Therapy for Adults With Type 1 Diabetes

### Recommendations
- **9.1** Treat most adults with type 1 diabetes with continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion or multiple daily doses of prandial (injected or inhaled) and basal insulin. **A**
- **9.2** For most adults with type 1 diabetes, insulin analogs (or inhaled insulin) are preferred over injectable human insulins to minimize hypoglycemia risk. **A**
- **9.3** To improve glycemic outcomes and quality of life and to minimize hypoglycemia risk, most adults with type 1 diabetes should receive education on how to match mealtime insulin doses to carbohydrate intake and fat and protein intake depending on the person’s or caregiver’s needs or preferences. They should also be taught how to modify the insulin dose (correction dose) based on concurrent glycemia, glycemic trends (if available), sick-day management, and anticipated physical activity. **B**
- **9.4** Insulin treatment plans and insulin-taking behaviors should be reevaluated at regular intervals (e.g., every 3–6 months) and adjusted to incorporate specific factors that affect choice of treatment and ensure achievement of individualized glycemic goals. **E**

### Insulin Therapy
Insulin replacement plans typically consist of basal insulin, mealtime insulin, and correction insulin. Basal insulin includes NPH insulin, long-acting insulin analogs, and continuous delivery of rapid-acting insulin via an insulin pump. In people with type 1 diabetes, treatment with analog insulins is associated with less hypoglycemia and weight gain and lower A1C compared with injectable human insulins.

Integration of continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) into the treatment plan soon after diagnosis improves glycemic outcomes, decreases hypoglycemic events, and improves quality of life. Its use is now considered standard of care for most people with type 1 diabetes. Automated insulin delivery (AID) systems are preferred for individuals with type 1 diabetes who can use them safely.

Typical multidose insulin treatment plans for adults with type 1 diabetes combine premeal prandial insulin with a longer-acting basal insulin. Total daily insulin requirements can be estimated based on weight, with typical doses ranging from 0.4 to 1 unit/kg/day.

### Insulin Administration Technique
Proper insulin administration includes injection into appropriate body areas, site rotation, avoidance of intramuscular delivery, and use of 4-mm pen needles when appropriate. Lipohypertrophy can contribute to erratic insulin absorption and increased glycemic variability.

### Noninsulin Treatments for Type 1 Diabetes
Adjunctive noninsulin agents include pramlintide, metformin, GLP-1 RAs, and SGLT2 inhibitors, with variable effects on A1C, weight, and risk of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). Sotagliflozin is contraindicated in type 1 diabetes due to DKA risk.

## Surgical Treatment of Type 1 Diabetes
Pancreas and islet transplantation can normalize glucose levels but require lifelong immunosuppression. Donislecel was approved in 2023 for adults with type 1 diabetes who cannot reach A1C goals because of repeated severe hypoglycemia despite intensive management.

## Pharmacologic Therapy for Adults With Type 2 Diabetes

### Recommendations
- **9.5** A person-centered shared decision-making approach should guide the choice of glucose-lowering medications for adults with type 2 diabetes. Use medications that provide sufficient effectiveness to achieve and maintain intended treatment goals with consideration of effects on cardiovascular, kidney, weight, and other comorbidities; hypoglycemia risk; cost and access; adverse reactions; and individual preferences. **E**
- **9.6** Consider combination therapy in adults with type 2 diabetes for initial treatment to shorten time to attainment of individualized glycemic goals. **A**
- **9.7** In adults with type 2 diabetes and established or high risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, the treatment plan should include medications with demonstrated benefits to reduce cardiovascular events (e.g., GLP-1 RA and/or SGLT2 inhibitor) for glycemic management and comprehensive cardiovascular risk reduction irrespective of A1C. **A**
- **9.8** In adults with type 2 diabetes who have heart failure (HF), an SGLT2 inhibitor is recommended for both glycemic management and prevention of HF hospitalizations irrespective of A1C. **A**
- **9.9a** In adults with type 2 diabetes, obesity, and symptomatic HFpEF, the glucose-lowering treatment plan should include a dual GIP and GLP-1 RA with demonstrated benefits for HF-related symptoms and reduction in HF events irrespective of A1C. **A**
- **9.9b** In adults with type 2 diabetes, obesity, and symptomatic HFpEF, the treatment plan should include a GLP-1 RA with demonstrated benefits for HF-related symptoms and/or reduction in HF events irrespective of A1C. **A/B**
- **9.10** In adults with type 2 diabetes who have chronic kidney disease (CKD), an SGLT2 inhibitor or GLP-1 RA with demonstrated benefit should be used for both glycemic management and slowing progression of CKD and reduction in cardiovascular events irrespective of A1C. **A**
- **9.11** In adults with type 2 diabetes and advanced CKD (eGFR <30), a GLP-1 RA is preferred for glycemic management due to lower risk of hypoglycemia and cardiovascular event reduction. **B**
- **9.12** In adults with type 2 diabetes, MASLD, and overweight or obesity, consider using a GLP-1 RA with demonstrated benefits in MASH or a dual GIP and GLP-1 RA with potential benefits in MASH. **A/B**
- **9.13a** In adults with type 2 diabetes and biopsy-proven MASH or high risk for liver fibrosis, a GLP-1 RA is preferred for glycemic management due to beneficial effects on MASH. **A**
- **9.14** Medication plan and medication-taking behavior should be reevaluated at regular intervals and adjusted as needed. **E**
- **9.15** Treatment modification for adults not meeting individualized treatment goals should not be delayed. **A**
- **9.16** Choice of modification should consider individualized glycemic and weight goals, comorbidities, and hypoglycemia risk. **A**
- **9.17** When initiating a new glucose-lowering medication, reassess the need for/dose of higher hypoglycemia-risk agents. **A**
- **9.18** Concurrent use of DPP-4 inhibitors with a GLP-1 RA or dual GIP/GLP-1 RA is not recommended due to lack of additional benefit. **B**
- **9.19** In adults who have not achieved individualized weight goals, additional weight management interventions are recommended. **A**
- **9.20** Initiation of insulin should be considered regardless of background therapy or disease duration if symptoms of hyperglycemia are present or A1C >10% or blood glucose ≥300 mg/dL. **E**
- **9.21** In adults without severe hyperglycemia, GLP-1–based therapy is preferred to insulin for initial or add-on therapy. **A**
- **9.22** If insulin is used, combination therapy with a GLP-1 RA is recommended for greater glycemic effectiveness and beneficial weight/hypoglycemia effects. **A**
- **9.23** In adults initiating insulin therapy, continue glucose-lowering agents unless contraindicated. **A**

### Choice of Glucose-Lowering Therapy
Metformin is effective, safe, inexpensive, and reduces microvascular and cardiovascular risks. Gastrointestinal side effects can be mitigated by gradual titration or extended-release formulations. Metformin should not be started if eGFR <45 and should be stopped if eGFR <30.

Comparative effectiveness: insulin, select GLP-1 RAs, and tirzepatide achieve the largest A1C reductions; DPP-4 inhibitors produce the smallest reductions. When A1C is ≥1.5% above goal, dual-combination or more potent agents should be considered.

### Glucose-Lowering Therapy for People With Cardiovascular Disease or Risk Factors
For people with type 2 diabetes and established ASCVD or indicators of high ASCVD risk, HF, or CKD, an SGLT2 inhibitor and/or GLP-1 RA is recommended independent of A1C.

### Glucose-Lowering Therapy for People With Chronic Kidney Disease
Preferred medications for CKD are GLP-1 RAs and SGLT2 inhibitors. Metformin should not be started if eGFR <45 and stopped if eGFR <30. Individuals with advanced CKD are at high risk for hypoglycemia if treated with insulin or sulfonylureas.

### Glucose-Lowering Therapy for People With Metabolic Comorbidities
For obesity/MASLD/MASH, prioritize therapies with favorable weight and liver effects: GLP-1 RAs, dual GIP/GLP-1 RA, and pioglitazone when appropriate. Metabolic surgery is very effective for weight and glycemic goals.

### Insulin Therapy
Basal insulin is the most convenient initial insulin treatment; start doses based on weight and degree of hyperglycemia. Long-acting analogs reduce nocturnal hypoglycemia compared with NPH. When intensifying to combination injectable therapy, consider GLP-1 RA or dual GIP/GLP-1 RA before prandial insulin.

## Additional Recommendations for All Individuals With Diabetes

### Recommendations
- **9.24** Include healthy behaviors, DSMES, avoidance of therapeutic inertia, and social determinants of health as essential components of management. **A**
- **9.25** Use of CGM is recommended at diabetes onset and anytime thereafter for adults on insulin therapy, on noninsulin therapies that can cause hypoglycemia, and on any treatment where CGM aids management. **A/B**
- **9.26** Monitor for signs of overbasalization during insulin therapy and reevaluate promptly when suspected. **E**
- **9.27** Automated insulin delivery systems should be offered to all adults with type 1 and type 2 diabetes on insulin depending on needs and preferences. **A**
- **9.28** Glucagon should be prescribed for all individuals taking insulin or at high risk for hypoglycemia. **A**
- **9.29** Routinely assess financial obstacles to diabetes management and collaboratively implement cost-reduction strategies. **E**
- **9.30** In adults with cost-related barriers, consider lower-cost medications within the context of their risks. **E**
